Mobile physiotherapy in the places daily life happens

Home and community-based physiotherapy can help when function, safety, equipment, fatigue, respiratory needs or care routines are best understood in the person's usual environment.

Why mobile physiotherapy can be useful

For people with neurological, respiratory or complex disability needs, clinic-based assessment may not show the full picture. A home or community visit can make it easier to understand how movement, fatigue, access, equipment and support routines work in real life.

This does not mean every referral needs mobile physiotherapy. Suitability depends on location, funding pathway, clinical need, consent, risk and availability.

Senior clinician-led care

Clinical approach

A practical, clinically cautious approach led by a senior physiotherapist.

Discuss suitability

Home and community assessment

Assessment considers how mobility, transfers, access, fatigue, equipment and support routines work in the places the person actually uses.

Function, safety, risk and goals

Therapy planning is guided by the person's functional priorities, known risks, clinical presentation and achievable next steps.

Carer and support-worker training

Where appropriate, training can focus on safer routines, mobility support, transfer practice, pacing and consistency of care.

Documentation and communication

Reports, recommendations and referrer updates can be provided where clinically appropriate and with consent to share relevant information.

What can be reviewed at home or in the community

Mobile assessment focuses on the tasks, risks and routines that matter day to day.

Real-world mobility

Walking, wheelchair mobility, endurance, balance, access barriers and community participation.

Transfers and daily routines

Bed, chair, toilet, shower, vehicle and support-worker assisted transfers.

Fatigue and respiratory needs

Pacing, physical capacity, respiratory change and escalation considerations in neurological disability.

Equipment and positioning

Mobility aids, seating, standing, positioning, pressure-care routines and equipment progression.

Support-worker training

Practical education around safe routines, movement support, risk awareness and consistency of care.

Documentation

Where clinically appropriate, recommendations can be documented for referrers, families and care teams.

A practical pathway

The first step is usually a brief suitability check before appointments or reports are discussed in detail.

  • Confirm suburb, funding pathway, consent and broad referral reason.
  • Clarify the main functional issue, risk, urgency and relevant care-team contacts.
  • Arrange assessment, therapy, training, liaison or documentation only where clinically appropriate.

Information that helps

Useful referral details include the person's suburb, preferred contact, funding pathway, current mobility or transfer concern, fatigue or respiratory issues, equipment needs, known risks and whether support-worker training or documentation may be required.
